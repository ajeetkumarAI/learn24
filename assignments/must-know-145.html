01NLP Basics & Text Preprocessing

1
What is NLP? Give 3 real-world applications you have seen in production.

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the branch of AI that enables computers to understand, interpret and generate human language (text and speech). It combines linguistics, machine learning and deep learning.

Real-world applications:

  • Chatbots and virtual assistants (customer support bots, ChatGPT-style assistants)
  • Sentiment analysis of product reviews / social media
  • Machine translation (Google Translate)
  • Spam and fraud-message detection, email classification
  • Document search, question answering and summarization (RAG systems)

What are the main challenges in NLP?
  • Ambiguity – the same word/sentence can have many meanings.
  • Context and long-range dependencies – meaning depends on earlier text.
  • Sarcasm, irony and idioms – "Great, another Monday!" is negative.
  • Spelling mistakes, slang, emojis, code-mixing (Hinglish).
  • Out-of-vocabulary words and new terminology.

  • Domain shift – a model trained on news fails on medical text.
  • Bias in training data, and hallucination in generative models.

📌 From Assignment 1 · Q3

3
What is tokenization? Compare word-level, character-level and subword tokenization.

Tokenization splits text into smaller units called tokens, which are then mapped to IDs for the model.

  • Word-level: "I love NLP" → ["I", "love", "NLP"]. Simple, but huge vocabulary and OOV problem.
  • Character-level: ["I", " ", "l", "o", ...]. Tiny vocabulary, no OOV, but very long sequences and weak meaning per token.
  • Subword-level (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece): "unhappiness" → ["un", "happi", "ness"]. Balanced vocabulary size (30k–200k), rare words are split into known pieces, so no OOV. All modern Transformers/LLMs use subword tokenization.

📌 From Assignment 1 · Q8

4
What is stemming and how is it different from lemmatization? When would you prefer each?
Asked: NLP interview rounds

Both reduce a word to a base form.

  • Stemming chops suffixes with rules (Porter, Snowball). Fast but crude; output may not be a real word: "studies" → "studi", "running" → "run".
  • Lemmatization uses vocabulary + grammar (POS) to return the dictionary form (lemma): "studies" → "study", "better" → "good" (adjective), "was" → "be".

Prefer stemming for speed on large data (search indexing, simple classifiers). Prefer lemmatization when meaning/readability matters (chatbots, topic modelling, information extraction).

📌 From Assignment 1 · Q9

5
What are subword tokenization methods? Explain BPE, WordPiece and SentencePiece briefly.

Subword tokenization splits rare words into frequent smaller units, so the vocabulary stays small and there are no OOV words.

  • BPE (Byte Pair Encoding) – starts with characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent pair of symbols until the vocabulary size is reached. Used by GPT-2/3/4, Llama 3, RoBERTa (byte-level BPE).
  • WordPiece – similar to BPE but merges the pair that maximizes the likelihood of the training data (score = freq(ab) / (freq(a)·freq(b))). Continuation pieces are prefixed with "##". Used by BERT, DistilBERT.
  • SentencePiece – a library that treats text as a raw stream (spaces become "▁"), so it is language-independent and needs no pre-tokenization. Implements BPE or Unigram LM (starts with a big vocabulary and prunes tokens that least reduce likelihood). Used by T5, ALBERT, XLNet, Llama 1/2, Mistral.

📌 From Assignment 1 · Q17

7
In real-time data users type "Lunch", "lunch" and "LUNCH". Why do we lowercase text, and when should you NOT lowercase?
Asked: NLP interview rounds

Why lowercase: so that different surface forms of the same word map to one token/feature. Without it, "Lunch", "lunch" and "LUNCH" become three different vocabulary entries → bigger, sparser vocabulary, fewer examples per word, worse generalization for BoW/TF-IDF/Word2Vec models. Users at inference time type in any case, so training and inference must be normalized the same way.

Do NOT lowercase when case carries meaning:

  • NER: "Apple" (company) vs "apple" (fruit); "US" vs "us".
  • Acronyms, product codes, tickers, IDs ("SBI", "PAN", "IT" department).
  • Sentiment/intent where shouting matters ("I am ANGRY").
  • Transformer/LLM pipelines with cased models – the tokenizer and model were trained on cased text; just pass raw text.

Rule: whatever you do in training, do exactly the same in the inference pipeline.

📌 From Assignment 1 · Q22

8
Users will type stopwords at inference time. If you remove them during training, what happens? When should you remove stopwords and when not? Give practical applications.
Asked: NLP interview rounds

Preprocessing must be identical at training and inference – if training removed stopwords, the inference pipeline must remove them too, otherwise the input distribution differs and accuracy drops. The bigger risk is losing meaning: "not", "no", "never", "without" are stopwords in NLTK.

Remove stopwords when: using classical BoW/TF-IDF models for topic-level tasks – document classification by topic, keyword extraction, topic modelling (LDA), search indexing/keyword search – where they add noise and dimensionality.

Keep stopwords when:

  • Sentiment analysis ("not good" ≠ "good").
  • Question answering, chatbots, intent detection ("where", "how", "can I").
  • Machine translation, summarization, text generation, NER.
  • Any Transformer/LLM/RAG pipeline (they need full grammar and context).

Use a custom stopword list that keeps negations if you must remove them.

02Text Representation & Word Embeddings

9
What is the Bag-of-Words model? What are its limitations?

Bag-of-Words (BoW) represents a document as the count (or presence) of each vocabulary word, ignoring grammar and word order. Example – vocabulary [I, love, NLP, hate]: "I love NLP" → [1, 1, 1, 0].

Limitations:

  • Loses word order: "dog bites man" = "man bites dog".
  • No semantics: "good" and "great" are unrelated dimensions.
  • High-dimensional and sparse (vocab can be 100k+).
  • Cannot handle OOV words.
  • Frequent words dominate unless weighted (→ TF-IDF).

📌 From Assignment 2 · Q2

10
What is TF-IDF? How do you calculate TF, IDF and TF-IDF? Work out a small example by hand.
Asked: NLP interview rounds

TF-IDF weights a word by how important it is to a document relative to the whole corpus.

  • TF(t, d) = (count of t in d) / (total words in d)
  • IDF(t) = log( N / df(t) ), where N = number of documents, df = number of documents containing t
  • TF-IDF = TF × IDF

Worked example (log base 10) – 3 documents: D1 "the cat sat", D2 "the dog sat", D3 "the cat ran".

  • Word "cat" in D1: TF = 1/3; df = 2 → IDF = log(3/2) = 0.176 → TF-IDF = 0.059
  • Word "the" in D1: TF = 1/3; df = 3 → IDF = log(3/3) = 0 → TF-IDF = 0 (common word, no information)
  • Word "ran" in D3: TF = 1/3; df = 1 → IDF = log(3) = 0.477 → TF-IDF = 0.159 (rare word, high weight)

(scikit-learn uses a smoothed version: idf = ln((1+N)/(1+df)) + 1, then L2-normalizes the vector.)

📌 From Assignment 2 · Q4

11
What are word embeddings? How are they better than one-hot / BoW vectors?

Word embeddings are dense, low-dimensional vectors (e.g. 100–1024 numbers) learned from data, where words with similar meaning have similar vectors.

Better than one-hot/BoW because:

  • Dense (300 dims vs 100k sparse dims) → less memory, better generalization.
  • Capture semantics: cos(king, queen) is high; famous analogy king − man + woman ≈ queen.
  • Similar words share information, so the model generalizes to words it saw less often.
  • Can be pre-trained on huge corpora and reused (transfer learning).

📌 From Assignment 2 · Q5

12
Explain Word2Vec and its two architectures (CBOW and Skip-gram). What is the difference?
Asked: NLP interview rounds

Word2Vec is a shallow neural network (one hidden layer) that learns word vectors by predicting words from their neighbours within a context window. The learned hidden-layer weights are the embeddings.

  • CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words): predicts the target word from the surrounding context words. Context "the cat __ on the mat" → "sat". Faster, works well for frequent words.
  • Skip-gram: predicts the context words from the target word. "sat" → "the", "cat", "on", "mat". Slower, but better for rare words and small datasets.

Training tricks: negative sampling and hierarchical softmax avoid computing softmax over the whole vocabulary.

📌 From Assignment 2 · Q11

14
Explain cosine similarity and why it is important in NLP.

Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors: cos(θ) = (A · B) / (‖A‖ × ‖B‖), ranging from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (same direction).

Why important in NLP:

  • It measures direction (meaning), not magnitude (length), so a long and a short document on the same topic still score high.
  • Works well for high-dimensional sparse and dense vectors.
  • It is the default metric for semantic search, RAG retrieval, recommendation, duplicate detection and clustering.

📌 From Assignment 2 · Q12

15
Euclidean distance vs cosine similarity — when would you use which?
  • Euclidean distance depends on vector magnitude. A long document has a larger count vector than a short one, so they look far apart even if they are about the same topic.
  • Cosine similarity depends only on the angle (direction), so it ignores length.

Use cosine for text, embeddings, semantic search (most cases). Use Euclidean when magnitude is meaningful (e.g. K-means on normalized numeric features, image features). Note: for L2-normalized vectors, ranking by Euclidean distance and by cosine give the same order.

📌 From Assignment 2 · Q14

16
Why can averaging Word2Vec vectors to make a sentence vector lose information? What is better?
Asked: NLP interview rounds

Averaging (mean pooling of word vectors) is a simple baseline, but:

  • Word order is lost: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" get the same vector.
  • Negation and composition are lost: "not good" ≈ average of "not" and "good", which stays close to "good".
  • Important words get diluted by many common/filler words in long sentences.
  • Each word has one static vector, so polysemy ("bank") is not resolved.
  • OOV words are simply missing.

Better options: TF-IDF–weighted averaging (down-weights common words), SIF embeddings, Doc2Vec, and today mainly Sentence Transformers / embedding models (SBERT, BGE, E5, OpenAI embeddings) that are trained specifically to produce sentence-level vectors with context and word order.

📌 From Assignment 2 · Q20

17
What are Sentence Transformers (SBERT)? Why are they better for semantic search than averaged word vectors or BERT's raw [CLS] vector?
Asked: NLP interview rounds

Sentence Transformers (SBERT) are Transformer encoders (BERT/MPNet/MiniLM) fine-tuned with a siamese / bi-encoder setup on sentence pairs (NLI, paraphrase, question–answer pairs) using contrastive losses, so that semantically similar sentences produce embeddings with high cosine similarity.

Why better:

  • Raw BERT [CLS] or mean-pooled BERT vectors were not trained for similarity – their cosine scores are poorly calibrated (often worse than GloVe averages for similarity tasks).
  • Averaged Word2Vec ignores order and context.
  • SBERT encodes the whole sentence in context into one vector that directly supports cosine search, clustering and RAG retrieval; it is fast because documents are encoded once and compared with dot products.

print(util.cos_sim(q, d))   # first document scores much higher

18
Explain embeddings to a non-technical person without using the word "vector".
Asked: Deloitte

An embedding is like giving every word or sentence a position on a giant map of meaning. Things that mean similar things are placed close together – 'refund', 'money back' and 'return payment' sit in the same neighbourhood, while 'office timings' is far away. The computer learns these positions by reading huge amounts of text and noticing which words appear in similar situations. So when a customer asks 'How do I get my money back?', we look up where that question lands on the map and pick the documents placed nearest to it – even if they use completely different words like 'refund policy'. That's how the system finds answers by meaning, not just by matching exact words.

03Neural Network Fundamentals

19
How does a neural network work? Explain with a single neuron first.

Single neuron: takes inputs x₁…xₙ, multiplies each by a weight, adds a bias, and passes the result through an activation function: z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ + b, output a = f(z)

Network: neurons are arranged in layers – input layer → hidden layers → output layer. Each layer's outputs are the next layer's inputs.

Learning:

1. Forward pass – compute predictions.

2. Loss – compare predictions with true labels.

3. Backpropagation – compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight (chain rule).

4. Update – an optimizer adjusts weights to reduce the loss.

5. Repeat over many batches and epochs until the loss stops decreasing. Non-linear activations let the network learn complex, non-linear patterns.

📌 From Assignment 3 · Q1

20
Explain the learning rate. What happens if it is too high or too low?

The learning rate (η) controls how big a step the optimizer takes in the direction of the negative gradient.

  • Too high: the updates overshoot the minimum, the loss oscillates or diverges (becomes NaN).
  • Too low: training is very slow, may get stuck in a poor local minimum/plateau, and needs many epochs.
  • Good practice: start with typical values (Adam: 1e-3; fine-tuning Transformers: 1e-5 to 5e-5; LoRA: 1e-4 to 2e-4), use a learning-rate finder, warm-up and schedulers (decay).

📌 From Assignment 3 · Q5

21
Explain forward propagation and backpropagation.

Forward propagation: input data passes layer by layer. For each layer, z = W·x + b and a = f(z). The last layer gives the prediction ŷ, and the loss L(y, ŷ) is computed.

Backpropagation: computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight using the chain rule, starting from the output layer and moving backward: ∂L/∂w = ∂L/∂a × ∂a/∂z × ∂z/∂w Each layer reuses the gradients already computed for the layer after it, which makes it efficient. The optimizer then uses these gradients to update weights. Frameworks (PyTorch autograd, TensorFlow GradientTape) do this automatically.

📌 From Assignment 3 · Q6

22
What are the different types of optimizers (SGD, Momentum, RMSProp, Adam, AdamW)?
  • SGD – basic gradient descent on mini-batches.
  • SGD with Momentum – adds a velocity term to accelerate and smooth updates.
  • Nesterov Accelerated Gradient – momentum that "looks ahead".
  • AdaGrad – per-parameter learning rate, decays with accumulated squared gradients (good for sparse data, but LR shrinks too much).
  • RMSProp – fixes AdaGrad using an exponential moving average of squared gradients.
  • Adam – Momentum + RMSProp (first and second moment estimates with bias correction). Default choice for most problems.
  • AdamW – Adam with decoupled weight decay; standard for Transformers/LLMs.
  • Others: Adadelta, Nadam, Lion, Adafactor (memory-efficient for large models).

📌 From Assignment 3 · Q8

23
What is the use of an activation function? What happens without one?

An activation function adds non-linearity, so the network can learn complex patterns. It also controls the output range (e.g. probabilities with sigmoid/softmax).

Without activation functions, every layer is a linear transformation, and a stack of linear layers collapses into one linear layer – a 100-layer network would be no more powerful than linear/logistic regression and could not learn non-linear patterns like XOR.

📌 From Assignment 3 · Q13

24
Which loss function should be used for regression, binary classification, multi-class and multi-label problems?
  • Regression: MSE (penalizes large errors), MAE (robust to outliers), Huber (mix of both). Output: linear.
  • Binary classification: Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE). Output: 1 neuron + sigmoid.
  • Multi-class (one label per sample): Categorical Cross-Entropy (one-hot labels) or Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy (integer labels). Output: N neurons + softmax.
  • Multi-label (several labels per sample): Binary Cross-Entropy per class. Output: N neurons + sigmoid.
  • Others: Hinge loss (SVM-style), KL divergence (distillation), contrastive / triplet loss (embeddings), Focal loss (heavy class imbalance, e.g. fraud detection).
  • LLM pre-training = cross-entropy on next-token prediction.

📌 From Assignment 3 · Q16

25
What are vanishing and exploding gradients? Why do they occur and how do you fix them?
  • Vanishing gradients: gradients become extremely small as they are propagated back through many layers (or time steps), so early layers learn very slowly or not at all.
  • Exploding gradients: gradients become extremely large, causing huge weight updates, unstable training and NaN loss.

Why they occur: backprop multiplies many derivatives together (chain rule). If they are < 1 (sigmoid/tanh derivatives, small weights) the product shrinks exponentially; if > 1 (large weights) it grows exponentially. Very deep networks and RNNs over long sequences are most affected.

Fixes:

  • ReLU-family activations instead of sigmoid/tanh.
  • Proper weight initialization (He/Xavier).
  • Batch Normalization / Layer Normalization.
  • Residual (skip) connections (ResNet, Transformers).
  • LSTM/GRU instead of vanilla RNN.
  • Gradient clipping (for exploding gradients).
  • Lower learning rate.

04Sequence Models: RNN, LSTM, GRU

27
What is the difference between an RNN and an ANN?
  • ANN (feed-forward) – input flows in one direction; each input is processed independently; fixed input size; no memory.
  • RNN – has a loop: the hidden state from the previous time step is fed back with the current input, h_t = f(W_xh·x_t + W_hh·h_(t−1) + b). It has memory, processes sequences of any length, and shares the same weights across time steps.

Use ANN for tabular data; RNN for sequential data (text, speech, time series).

📌 From Assignment 4 · Q1

28
Why do RNNs suffer from vanishing gradients and how can it be solved?
Asked: NLP interview rounds

During BPTT, the gradient for early time steps is a product of many terms involving W_hh and the derivative of tanh (≤ 1). Over long sequences this product shrinks exponentially, so the gradient from words far back becomes ~0. Result: the RNN cannot learn long-term dependencies ("The man who lived in the house that ... was tall").

Solutions:

  • LSTM / GRU – gated architectures with an additive memory path.
  • ReLU activations + identity/orthogonal initialization.
  • Gradient clipping (for exploding gradients).
  • Skip connections, layer normalization.
  • Ultimately attention / Transformers, which connect every position directly.

📌 From Assignment 4 · Q6

29
Explain the LSTM cell: cell state, hidden state and the three gates (forget, input, output).
Asked: NLP interview rounds

An LSTM cell has two states and three gates:

  • Cell state (C_t) – the long-term memory "conveyor belt"; information flows along it with only small, controlled changes.
  • Hidden state (h_t) – short-term memory / output at the current step.
  • Forget gate f_t – decides what to remove from the cell state (sigmoid: 0 = forget, 1 = keep).
  • Input gate i_t – decides what new information to add, together with the candidate values C̃_t (tanh).
  • Output gate o_t – decides what part of the cell state is output as the hidden state.

Gates use sigmoid (values 0–1) to act like soft switches.

📌 From Assignment 4 · Q7

30
How does LSTM address vanishing gradients and long-term dependencies?

The key is the additive cell-state update: C_t = f_t ⊙ C_(t−1) + i_t ⊙ C̃_t.

  • The gradient flowing back through the cell state is multiplied by the forget gate f_t, not by repeated weight matrices and tanh derivatives.
  • When f_t ≈ 1, the gradient passes almost unchanged across many time steps (the "constant error carousel"), so it does not vanish.
  • The gates learn what to keep for a long time (e.g. the subject of a sentence) and what to forget, so important information can be carried across long sequences.

LSTMs reduce, but do not completely eliminate, the problem for very long sequences (hundreds+ of steps).

📌 From Assignment 4 · Q9

31
What is a GRU? Explain its gates (update, reset). How does it differ from LSTM?

A GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) is a simplified LSTM with two gates and only one state (hidden state, no separate cell state).

  • Update gate z_t – how much of the past to keep vs how much new information to add (combines LSTM's forget + input gates).
  • Reset gate r_t – how much of the previous hidden state to use when computing the new candidate.

Equations: z_t = σ(W_z·[h_(t−1), x_t]); r_t = σ(W_r·[h_(t−1), x_t]); h̃_t = tanh(W·[r_t ⊙ h_(t−1), x_t]); h_t = (1 − z_t) ⊙ h_(t−1) + z_t ⊙ h̃_t Differences from LSTM: 2 gates vs 3, no cell state, ~25% fewer parameters, faster to train, often similar accuracy.

📌 From Assignment 4 · Q11

32
Why did Transformers replace RNNs/LSTMs for most NLP tasks?
  • Parallelization: RNNs process tokens one by one; Transformers process the whole sequence at once → much faster training on GPUs.
  • Long-range dependencies: self-attention connects any two tokens directly (path length 1), whereas RNNs must pass information through many steps.
  • No vanishing gradient through time.
  • Scalability: Transformers scale to billions of parameters and huge datasets; this enabled pre-training (BERT, GPT) and transfer learning.
  • Better results on almost every NLP benchmark.

(RNNs are still used in small/edge or streaming settings, and new recurrent-style models like Mamba/state-space models are being explored for long contexts.)

05Attention & Transformer Architecture

33
What is attention and why was it needed (the bottleneck problem)?

Attention lets the model, while producing each output, look at all input positions and focus on the most relevant ones by computing a weighted sum of their representations, where the weights are learned relevance scores.

Why it was needed – the bottleneck problem: in RNN seq2seq the whole input sentence had to be squeezed into one fixed-size vector. Long sentences lost information, and translation quality dropped sharply with length. With attention (Bahdanau 2014), the decoder gets a different context vector at every step, built directly from all encoder states. It also handles alignment (which source word matches which target word) and gives some interpretability (attention maps).

📌 From Assignment 5 · Q3

34
What is self-attention? Explain Query, Key and Value with an intuitive example.
Asked: Google, NLP rounds

Self-attention lets every token in a sequence look at every other token in the same sequence and build a context-aware representation of itself. Each token embedding x is projected into three vectors with learned matrices: Q = xW_Q, K = xW_K, V = xW_V.

  • Query – "what am I looking for?"
  • Key – "what do I contain / what can I be matched on?"
  • Value – "the information I give if selected."

Intuition (library search): your query is the search text, keys are the book titles, values are the book contents. You compare the query with all keys, and read a weighted mix of the matching contents.

Example: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired." The query for "it" matches strongly with the key of "animal", so the new vector for "it" contains information from "animal".

📌 From Assignment 5 · Q5

35
What is Scaled Dot-Product Attention? Write the formula. Why do we divide by √dₖ?

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √dₖ ) · V Steps: (1) dot product of each query with all keys = similarity scores; (2) divide by √dₖ; (3) (optional mask); (4) softmax → attention weights that sum to 1; (5) weighted sum of value vectors.

Why divide by √dₖ: for vectors with dₖ dimensions (unit-variance entries), the dot product has variance dₖ, so with dₖ = 64 the scores become large. Large values push softmax into regions where one weight ≈ 1 and others ≈ 0, making gradients extremely small. Scaling by √dₖ keeps the variance ≈ 1 and keeps training stable.

📌 From Assignment 5 · Q6

36
What is multi-head attention and why is it better than a single head?

Multi-head attention runs several attention operations ("heads") in parallel, each with its own W_Q, W_K, W_V projecting to a smaller dimension (d_model / h). Outputs of all heads are concatenated and projected with W_O: MultiHead(Q,K,V) = Concat(head₁, …, head_h)·W_O (BERT-base: 12 heads × 64 dims = 768.)

Why better than one head: each head can learn a different type of relationship – one head for syntax (subject–verb), one for coreference ("it" → "animal"), one for nearby words, etc. A single head would average all of these into one distribution. Total compute is about the same as one full-size head.

📌 From Assignment 5 · Q7

37
How do Transformers account for word order? Explain positional encoding using sine and cosine.
Asked: NLP interview rounds

Self-attention is permutation-invariant – without extra information, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical. So Transformers add a positional encoding vector to each token embedding before the first layer.

Sinusoidal formula (original Transformer): PE(pos, 2i) = sin( pos / 10000^(2i/d_model) ) PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos / 10000^(2i/d_model) ) Even dimensions use sine, odd use cosine; each dimension pair has a different wavelength (from 2π to 10000·2π), like the hands of a clock running at different speeds.

Why sine/cosine: unique encoding for every position; values bounded in [−1, 1]; PE(pos + k) is a linear function of PE(pos), so the model can easily learn relative positions; no parameters; can extrapolate to longer sequences than seen in training.

📌 From Assignment 5 · Q9

38
What is masked (causal) attention and why is it used in the decoder?

Masked (causal) self-attention prevents a position from attending to future positions. Before softmax, scores for future tokens are set to −∞ using an upper-triangular mask, so their attention weights become 0.

Why: the decoder generates text left-to-right. During training we feed the whole target sentence at once (for parallelism), so without the mask the model could "cheat" by looking at the next word it is supposed to predict. The mask makes training match inference, where future tokens do not exist yet. All GPT-style decoder-only LLMs use causal masking. (A separate padding mask hides [PAD] tokens.)

39
Explain the complete Transformer architecture end-to-end (draw it).
Asked: TCS, Google

Encoder (N = 6 identical layers):

1. Input tokens → token embeddings (× √d_model) + positional encodings.

2. Each layer: multi-head self-attention → Add & LayerNorm → feed-forward network → Add & LayerNorm.

3. Output: a contextual vector for every input token.

Decoder (N = 6 layers):

1. Shifted-right target tokens → embeddings + positional encodings.

2. Each layer: masked multi-head self-attention → Add & Norm → cross-attention (Q from decoder, K/V from encoder output) → Add & Norm → FFN → Add & Norm.

Output head: Linear → Softmax over vocabulary → next-token probabilities. Original base model: d_model = 512, 8 heads, d_ff = 2048, dropout 0.1, Adam with warm-up. Training uses teacher forcing; inference is autoregressive (generate one token, append, repeat). (Draw it: two stacks side by side, encoder output arrows feeding into every decoder layer's cross-attention.)

📌 From Assignment 5 · Q17

40
What is the time and memory complexity of self-attention with respect to sequence length?

For sequence length n and model dimension d:

  • Time: O(n² · d) – every token computes a score with every other token (the n × n attention matrix).
  • Memory: O(n²) for the attention matrix (per head, per layer), plus O(n·d) activations.

Doubling the context length makes attention 4× more expensive. This is why long-context models use Flash Attention (reduces memory to O(n) by not storing the full matrix), sliding-window/sparse attention, and KV caching during generation. (RNNs are O(n·d²) but sequential.)

📌 From Assignment 5 · Q18

41
How do LLMs understand words with multiple meanings, e.g. "bank" of a river vs a financial "bank"?
Asked: EPAM

Through contextual embeddings built by self-attention:

1. The token "bank" starts with the same input embedding in both sentences.

2. In every Transformer layer, self-attention lets "bank" attend to the surrounding words – "river", "water", "sat" in one sentence; "deposit", "loan", "money" in the other.

3. The weighted mix of those context vectors is added to the "bank" representation layer after layer, so by the upper layers the two "bank" vectors are completely different – one close to finance concepts, the other close to geography.

4. Multi-head attention lets different heads capture different cues (nearby words, syntax, topic). This is the key advantage over static embeddings like Word2Vec, which give "bank" one fixed vector.

📌 From Assignment 6 · Q1

43
Explain Multi-Head Attention (MHA), Multi-Query Attention (MQA) and Grouped-Query Attention (GQA).
  • MHA (Multi-Head Attention): every head has its own Q, K and V projections. Highest quality, but at inference the KV cache stores K and V for every head → large memory.
  • MQA (Multi-Query Attention): all heads have their own Q, but share a single K and a single V head. KV cache shrinks by the number of heads (e.g. 32×) → much faster decoding, but some quality loss and training instability. (PaLM, Falcon.)
  • GQA (Grouped-Query Attention): a middle ground – query heads are split into G groups; each group shares one K/V head. E.g. 32 query heads, 8 KV heads → 4× smaller KV cache with quality close to MHA. Used in Llama 2-70B, Llama 3, Mistral. GQA with G = 1 is MQA; G = number of heads is MHA.

📌 From Assignment 6 · Q3

44
What is the KV cache and why is it needed? How does it affect memory at long context lengths?

KV cache: during autoregressive generation, each new token must attend to all previous tokens. Without caching, the model would recompute keys and values for the entire prefix at every step (quadratic waste). The KV cache stores the K and V tensors of all previous tokens for every layer, so at each step only the new token's Q, K, V are computed and the new K, V are appended.

Benefit: generation becomes much faster (linear work per new token instead of reprocessing everything).

Memory cost: KV cache size = 2 (K and V) × layers × KV heads × head_dim × sequence length × batch size × bytes per value. Example: Llama-2-7B (32 layers, 32 heads × 128 dim, FP16) ≈ 0.5 MB per token → a 4,096-token context needs ~2 GB per sequence. At long contexts the KV cache can be larger than the model weights; solutions are GQA/MQA, KV-cache quantization, PagedAttention (vLLM), sliding windows.

📌 From Assignment 6 · Q5

45
What are the types of positional embeddings (absolute, learned, relative, RoPE, ALiBi)? Absolute vs relative?
  • Absolute sinusoidal (original Transformer) – fixed sine/cosine vectors added to embeddings.
  • Absolute learned (BERT, GPT-2) – a trainable embedding per position; cannot go beyond max length (512/1024).
  • Relative position (Transformer-XL, T5 relative bias) – encode the distance between tokens (i − j) inside attention instead of absolute positions.
  • RoPE – Rotary Position Embedding (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, GPT-NeoX) – rotates Q and K vectors by an angle proportional to their position, so the dot product depends only on relative distance.
  • ALiBi (BLOOM, MPT) – adds a linear penalty to attention scores proportional to distance; good length extrapolation.

Absolute vs relative: absolute embeddings encode "I am at position 5"; relative embeddings encode "this token is 3 positions before me". Relative methods generalize better to longer sequences and capture what really matters (distance between words), while absolute learned embeddings are limited to the trained length.

What is BERT? How is it bidirectional?
Asked: NLP interview rounds

BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) – Google, 2018 – is a stack of Transformer encoder layers pre-trained on large text (Wikipedia + BooksCorpus) and then fine-tuned for downstream tasks. BERT-base: 12 layers, 768 hidden, 12 heads, 110M parameters; BERT-large: 24 layers, 1024 hidden, 16 heads, 340M.

📌 From Assignment 6 · Q13

47
Which tasks are used for BERT pre-training (MLM and NSP)? Why did RoBERTa drop NSP?

1. Masked Language Modelling (MLM): 15% of tokens are selected; of these, 80% are replaced with [MASK], 10% with a random token, 10% left unchanged; the model predicts the original tokens. (The 80/10/10 split reduces the mismatch because [MASK] never appears during fine-tuning.)

2. Next Sentence Prediction (NSP): given sentence A and B, predict whether B actually follows A (50% real, 50% random) using the [CLS] output. Intended to help tasks like QA and NLI.

Why RoBERTa dropped NSP: experiments showed NSP did not help and sometimes hurt performance – the task was too easy (random sentences usually differ in topic) and it forced training on short segment pairs. 07LLM Fundamentals

48
What is a Large Language Model? How is it trained (pre-training → instruction tuning → alignment)?
Asked: Google

A Large Language Model (LLM) is a very large (billions of parameters) Transformer, usually decoder-only, trained to predict the next token on massive text data. Through this it learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and coding ability, and can perform many tasks through prompting.

Training stages:

1. Pre-training – self-supervised next-token prediction on trillions of tokens (web, books, code). Produces a base model that completes text. Most expensive stage (thousands of GPUs, weeks/months).

2. Instruction tuning / SFT – supervised fine-tuning on (instruction, response) pairs and multi-turn chats, so the model follows instructions and adopts a chat format.

3. Alignment / preference tuning – RLHF (reward model + PPO), DPO or similar methods using human (or AI) preference data, to make responses more helpful, honest and harmless. Recent models add reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks (maths, code) to improve reasoning.

📌 From Assignment 7 · Q2

50
What is RLHF? Explain the reward model and PPO. What is DPO and why is it simpler?
Asked: Google

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback):

1. SFT – fine-tune the base model on good demonstrations.

2. Reward model – humans compare pairs of responses to the same prompt (A better than B). Train a model to output a scalar score that predicts human preference.

3. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) – the LLM (policy) generates responses, the reward model scores them, and PPO updates the LLM to maximize reward, with a KL penalty that keeps it close to the SFT model (prevents "reward hacking" and gibberish). PPO clips updates for stability.

DPO (Direct Preference Optimization): skips the separate reward model and RL loop. It uses the preference pairs directly with a simple classification-style loss that increases the probability of the chosen response and decreases the rejected one, relative to a frozen reference model.

Why DPO is simpler: only two models (policy + reference) instead of four (policy, reference, reward, value), no sampling during training, stable like ordinary fine-tuning, fewer hyperparameters, cheaper. Variants: IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO.

51
What is a context window? What problems appear with very long contexts ("lost in the middle", cost, latency)?

The context window is the maximum number of tokens (prompt + generated output) the model can process at once – e.g. 8k, 128k, 200k or 1M+ tokens.

Problems with very long contexts:

  • "Lost in the middle" – models use information at the beginning and end of the context better than information buried in the middle.
  • Cost – API pricing is per token; huge prompts are expensive.
  • Latency – time-to-first-token grows with prompt length (attention is O(n²), plus KV-cache memory).
  • Distraction – irrelevant content can reduce accuracy; effective context is often shorter than the advertised limit.

Mitigations: RAG (send only relevant chunks), put key instructions at the start/end, summarization, prompt caching, re-ranking.

📌 From Assignment 7 · Q5

52
Explain decoding strategies: greedy, beam search, top-k, top-p (nucleus) and temperature.
Asked: TCS, EPAM, Google
  • Greedy decoding – always pick the highest-probability token. Fast, deterministic, but repetitive and can miss better sequences.
  • Beam search – keep the top-B partial sequences at each step and return the best overall. Good for translation/summarization; tends to be generic and repetitive for open-ended chat.
  • Temperature – divides logits by T before softmax. T < 1 → sharper, more deterministic; T > 1 → flatter, more random/creative; T → 0 ≈ greedy.
  • Top-k sampling – sample only from the k most likely tokens.
  • Top-p (nucleus) sampling – sample from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability ≥ p (e.g. 0.9); adapts the candidate set size to the model's confidence.

Other controls: repetition / frequency / presence penalties, min-p, max_new_tokens, stop sequences.

📌 From Assignment 7 · Q6

53
What is quantization (INT8, 4-bit, GPTQ, AWQ, GGUF)? What do you trade off?

Quantization stores model weights (and sometimes activations/KV cache) in lower precision – FP32 (4 bytes) → FP16/BF16 (2) → INT8 (1) → 4-bit (0.5 byte) – to reduce memory and speed up inference.

  • INT8 (LLM.int8()) – 8-bit with outlier handling; near-lossless.
  • 4-bit NF4 (bitsandbytes) – used by QLoRA for training on consumer GPUs.
  • GPTQ – post-training quantization that minimizes layer-wise error using second-order information; fast GPU inference.
  • AWQ – activation-aware; protects the ~1% most important weights; good quality at 4-bit.
  • GGUF – file format used by llama.cpp / Ollama for CPU and Apple-silicon inference, with many quant levels (Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q8_0).

Trade-off: less memory, lower cost and faster inference vs some loss in accuracy (small at 8-bit, noticeable at 3-bit and below, especially on maths/reasoning), plus calibration effort and hardware/kernel support.

What is hallucination in an LLM? What causes it and how do you reduce it?
Asked: TCS, Infosys, EPAM, Google

Hallucination – the model generates content that is fluent and confident but factually wrong, unsupported or made up (fake citations, wrong numbers, non-existent APIs).

Causes: LLMs are trained to produce *plausible* next tokens, not verified facts; gaps or errors in training data; knowledge cut-off; ambiguous prompts; sampling randomness; training that rewards answering over saying "I don't know".

How to reduce:

  • RAG – ground answers in retrieved documents and instruct "answer only from the context; say 'I don't know' otherwise".
  • Ask for citations/quotes from sources and verify them.
  • Lower temperature for factual tasks.
  • Clear, specific prompts; chain-of-thought or self-verification steps.
  • Tools (search, calculator, database) for facts and maths.
  • Fine-tuning on domain data; better/larger models.
  • Evaluation and guardrails: faithfulness checks (RAGAS), LLM-as-judge, human review for high-risk outputs.

What are autoregressive models?

Autoregressive models generate a sequence one token at a time, where each new token is predicted from all previously generated tokens: P(x) = Π P(x_t | x_1 … x_(t−1)). The generated token is appended to the input and the process repeats until an end token or max length. Examples: GPT family, Llama, Mistral (decoder-only LLMs); the decoder of T5/BART is also autoregressive. They use causal masking during training. Contrast: BERT (masked/non-autoregressive) cannot generate text naturally.

How do you summarize a document larger than the context window? Explain stuff, map_reduce and refine.

When a document is larger than the context window, split it into chunks and use one of these strategies:

  • Stuff – put all chunks into one prompt and summarize once. Simplest, one LLM call, best quality – but only works if everything fits in the context window.
  • Map-Reduce – Map: summarize each chunk independently (in parallel). Reduce: combine the chunk summaries into a final summary (recursively if still too long). Scales to very large documents and is fast; may lose connections between chunks.
  • Refine – summarize the first chunk, then iteratively update the running summary with each next chunk ("here is the existing summary, refine it with this new text"). Keeps context flowing, but is sequential (slow) and later chunks can dominate.

What is RAG? Explain its architecture (indexing pipeline + retrieval + generation) with a diagram.
Asked: TCS, PwC, Infosys, Google

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) gives an LLM access to external, up-to-date or private knowledge at query time: relevant passages are retrieved and inserted into the prompt, and the LLM answers using them. It reduces hallucination, enables citations, and avoids retraining when data changes.

Indexing pipeline (offline):

1. Load documents (PDFs, web pages, DB records).

2. Split into chunks.

3. Embed each chunk with an embedding model.

4. Store vectors + text + metadata in a vector database.

Retrieval + generation (online, per query):

5. Embed the user query with the same embedding model.

6. Retrieve the top-k most similar chunks (optionally hybrid search + re-ranking).

7. Augment the prompt: instructions + retrieved context + question.

8. Generate the answer with the LLM (with citations). Diagram: Docs → Chunker → Embeddings → Vector DB; Query → Embedding → Vector DB search → top-k chunks → Prompt → LLM → Answer.

What are chunking strategies (fixed, recursive, semantic, document-structure based)? How do you choose chunk size?
Asked: TCS, Deloitte, Google
  • Fixed-size chunking – split every N characters/tokens with overlap. Simple and fast; can cut sentences.
  • Recursive character chunking – split on paragraphs → lines → sentences → words to respect natural boundaries (good default).
  • Sentence-based – group whole sentences up to a size limit.
  • Semantic chunking – embed sentences and start a new chunk where the meaning changes (embedding similarity drops). Better coherence, more compute.
  • Document-structure based – split by headings/sections (Markdown, HTML), pages, tables, code functions; keep headings as metadata.
  • Advanced: parent–child chunks (small for retrieval, large for context), contextual chunk headers, agentic/LLM-based chunking.

Choosing chunk size: depends on the content and questions – typical 256–1,024 tokens with 10–20% overlap. Small chunks → precise retrieval but missing context; large chunks → more context but noisier embeddings and more tokens. Experiment and measure retrieval quality (context recall/precision) on a test set; also respect the embedding model's max input length.

81
How are indexes created in vector databases? Explain flat, IVF, PQ and HNSW.
  • Flat (brute-force) index – compares the query with every vector. 100% exact, but O(N) – fine up to ~100k vectors.
  • IVF (Inverted File Index) – clusters vectors with k-means into many lists (centroids). At query time, only the closest `nprobe` clusters are searched → much faster, approximate.
  • PQ (Product Quantization) – compresses vectors: split each vector into sub-vectors and replace each with the ID of its nearest centroid in a small codebook (e.g. 768 floats → 96 bytes). Huge memory savings, approximate distances. Often combined: IVF-PQ.
  • HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) – a multi-layer graph of vectors; search greedily walks the graph. Very fast and high recall; uses more memory. Default in most vector DBs.

These are Approximate Nearest Neighbour (ANN) indexes – they trade a little accuracy for large speed gains. (Others: LSH, Annoy trees, ScaNN, DiskANN.)

📌 From Assignment 11 · Q6

82
How does HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds) work?

HNSW builds a multi-layer proximity graph, similar to a skip list:

  • Layer 0 (bottom) contains all vectors, each linked to its M nearest neighbours.
  • Higher layers contain progressively fewer randomly selected nodes with long-range links (like express highways).

Search:

1. Start at an entry point in the top layer.

2. Greedily move to the neighbour closest to the query until no neighbour is closer.

3. Drop down one layer and repeat from that node.

4. In the bottom layer, do a wider search (beam of size ef_search) and return the top-k.

Insertion: a new vector gets a random max layer and is connected to its nearest neighbours in each layer.

Parameters: M (links per node – memory vs recall), ef_construction (build quality), ef_search (query accuracy vs speed). Complexity ≈ O(log N) per query; high recall (>95%) with millisecond latency; cost: high memory, slower inserts, deletes are tricky.

📌 From Assignment 11 · Q7

83
What is MMR (Maximal Marginal Relevance) and why use it?
Asked: Wipro

Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) selects results that are relevant to the query but different from each other. Algorithm: fetch `fetch_k` candidates (e.g. 20) by similarity, then pick results one by one, each time choosing the candidate that maximizes: MMR = λ · sim(query, doc) − (1 − λ) · max sim(doc, already_selected) λ = 1 → pure relevance; λ = 0 → maximum diversity (`lambda_mult` in LangChain).

Why use it: plain top-k often returns near-duplicate chunks (same paragraph from several versions of a document), wasting context-window space. MMR gives broader coverage of different aspects of the question.

What is hybrid search (BM25 + vector)? Why does it help?
Asked: Google

Hybrid search combines:

  • Keyword/lexical search (BM25) – exact term matching; excellent for names, IDs, codes, acronyms, rare terms ("error E-4021", "Form 16", product SKUs).
  • Vector/semantic search – matches meaning and paraphrases ("how to get money back" ↔ "refund policy").

Results are merged using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) or a weighted score: score = α·vector + (1 − α)·BM25.

Why it helps: each method covers the other's weakness – embeddings miss exact rare tokens, BM25 misses synonyms – so recall improves noticeably.

What is re-ranking? Explain cross-encoders and Cohere Rerank.
Asked: Wipro, Google

Re-ranking is a second stage after retrieval: retrieve a larger candidate set quickly (e.g. top-25 via vectors/BM25), then use a more accurate model to re-score each (query, document) pair and keep the best few (top-3–5) for the LLM.

  • Bi-encoder (embedding model): encodes query and document separately → fast, can pre-compute document vectors, but less precise.

  • Cohere Rerank – a hosted, multilingual cross-encoder re-ranking API (CohereRerank in LangChain).
from langchain_classic.retrievers import ContextualCompressionRetriever   # langchain<1.0:
    langchain.retrievers

What is an AI agent? How is it different from a chain?
Asked: EPAM, PwC, Google

An AI agent is a system where an LLM decides the control flow: given a goal, it reasons about what to do, chooses tools/actions, observes the results, and loops until the task is done.

  • Chain – a fixed, pre-defined sequence of steps written by the developer (prompt → LLM → parser, or retrieve → generate). Predictable, cheap, easy to test.
  • Agent – the sequence is decided dynamically by the LLM at runtime (which tool, how many steps, when to stop). Flexible for open-ended tasks, but less predictable, slower and costlier.

Rule of thumb: use a chain/workflow when the steps are known; use an agent only when the path genuinely depends on the input.

What are tools and how do they improve an LLM's capability? How does tool/function calling work?
Asked: TCS, Google

Tools are functions that an LLM can call to interact with the outside world – search the web, query a database, call an API, run code, do maths, send email. Each tool has a name, description and input schema.

4. The model uses the result to answer, or calls more tools.

What is MCP (Model Context Protocol) and what problem does it solve?
Asked: TCS, EPAM, Infosys, Google

MCP (Model Context Protocol) is an open standard (introduced by Anthropic in late 2024, now widely adopted by OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and IDEs) for connecting AI applications to external tools, data sources and prompts in a uniform way – often described as " USB-C for AI applications ".

Security: treat third-party servers as untrusted code; review permissions; beware tool poisoning and prompt injection.

108
When should you fine-tune an LLM and when should you not?
Asked: Infosys, Google

Fine-tune when:

  • You need a consistent style, tone or output format that prompting cannot reliably achieve.
  • A narrow, repetitive task (classification, extraction, routing) where a small fine-tuned model can replace a large expensive one → lower cost and latency.
  • Domain language/jargon the base model handles poorly (medical, legal, internal codes).
  • You have enough good-quality labelled examples (hundreds to thousands).
  • Data must stay on-prem with an open model.

Don't fine-tune when:

  • The problem is missing or changing knowledge → use RAG.
  • Good prompting or few-shot examples already work → start with prompting.
  • You have little/poor data, or no evaluation set.
  • Requirements change often (each change means retraining).

Order to try: prompt engineering → RAG → fine-tuning (or combine).

109
What is Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) and why is it needed?
Asked: TCS

PEFT (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning) adapts a large pre-trained model by training only a small number of (new or selected) parameters while keeping most original weights frozen. Methods: LoRA, QLoRA, adapters, prefix tuning, prompt tuning, (IA)³.

Why needed:

  • Full fine-tuning of a 7B+ model needs enormous GPU memory (~16 bytes/parameter with Adam → 100+ GB) and compute.
  • Storing a full copy of the model for every task/customer is expensive; PEFT adapters are only a few MB.
  • Less overfitting and less catastrophic forgetting on small datasets.
  • Makes fine-tuning possible on a single consumer/Colab GPU; often reaches quality close to full fine-tuning.

📌 From Assignment 13 · Q2

110
What are the challenges of full fine-tuning and how does LoRA overcome them?

Challenges of full fine-tuning:

  • Memory: gradients and optimizer states for all billions of parameters.
  • Compute/time and cost.
  • Storage: a full model copy (e.g. 14 GB) per fine-tuned task.
  • Catastrophic forgetting and overfitting on small datasets.

How LoRA overcomes them:

  • Freezes the original weights and trains only small low-rank matrices (often < 1% of parameters) → far fewer gradients and optimizer states → much less memory.
  • Faster training; can use bigger batches or smaller GPUs.
  • Adapters are tiny (MBs) – one base model can serve many task-specific adapters, swapped at runtime.
  • Original weights unchanged → less forgetting; after training, adapters can be merged into the weights, so there is no extra inference latency.

📌 From Assignment 13 · Q3

111
Explain how LoRA works. How are the weight matrices updated? (W + BA)

Idea: the weight change needed for a new task has a low intrinsic rank, so instead of learning a full update ΔW (d × k), LoRA learns it as the product of two small matrices:

W' = W₀ + ΔW = W₀ + (α / r) · B · A

  • W₀ (d × k) – frozen pre-trained weight.
  • A (r × k) – initialized with small random values; B (d × r) – initialized with zeros, so ΔW = 0 at the start (training begins from the original model).
  • r ≪ min(d, k), e.g. r = 8.

Forward pass: h = W₀x + (α/r)·B(Ax).

Update: during backprop, gradients are computed only for A and B; the optimizer updates them; W₀ never changes.

Parameter saving example: d = k = 4096 → full ΔW = 16.8M parameters; LoRA with r = 8 → 8 × (4096 + 4096) = 65,536 parameters (≈ 0.4%). After training: merge W = W₀ + (α/r)BA for zero inference overhead, or keep adapters separate.

📌 From Assignment 13 · Q4

112
LoRA vs QLoRA — what is the difference and when would you use each?

QLoRA = Quantized base model + LoRA.

  • LoRA: base model loaded in 16-bit (FP16/BF16); LoRA adapters trained on top.
  • QLoRA: base model loaded in 4-bit NF4 (NormalFloat-4) and frozen; LoRA adapters trained in 16-bit. Gradients flow through the de-quantized weights. Extra tricks: double quantization (quantize the quantization constants) and paged optimizers (avoid out-of-memory spikes).

Memory: QLoRA cuts base-model memory by ~4× → a 7B model trains on a single ~16 GB GPU (even free Colab T4), 65–70B on a single 48–80 GB GPU.

When to use:

  • LoRA – enough GPU memory; you want faster training and slightly better quality / no quantization error.
  • QLoRA – limited GPU memory or large models; accept ~20–40% slower training (de-quantization overhead) and a very small quality drop.

📌 From Assignment 14 · Q2

116
Explain faithfulness, answer relevancy, context precision and context recall.
  • Faithfulness – measures whether every claim in the answer can be inferred from the retrieved context. An LLM splits the answer into individual statements and checks each against the context. Score = supported claims / total claims (0–1). Low faithfulness = hallucination. Example: context says "refund within 7 days", answer says "refund within 30 days" → unfaithful.
  • Answer relevancy – measures how well the answer addresses the question (not whether it is true). RAGAS asks an LLM to generate possible questions from the answer and computes the average cosine similarity between them and the original question. Incomplete or off-topic/padded answers score low.
  • Context precision – measures whether the relevant retrieved chunks are ranked higher than irrelevant ones (a precision@k averaged over ranks). High = little noise at the top of the results.
  • Context recall – measures whether the retrieved context contains all the information needed to produce the ground-truth answer: each sentence of the reference answer is checked for whether it can be attributed to the context. Needs a reference answer. Low recall = retriever missed information.

📌 From Assignment 14 · Q3

117
How do you evaluate retrieval quality? Explain Precision@K, Recall@K, MRR (and nDCG).
Asked: TCS

Need a test set of queries with the known relevant chunks/documents.

  • Precision@K = (relevant chunks in top K) / K – how much of what we retrieved is useful (noise).
  • Recall@K = (relevant chunks in top K) / (all relevant chunks for that query) – did we find everything needed. Most important for RAG (missing context = wrong answer).
  • Hit rate@K – % of queries where at least one relevant chunk is in the top K.
  • MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) = average over queries of 1 / (rank of the first relevant result). Rank 1 → 1.0, rank 2 → 0.5, rank 4 → 0.25.
  • nDCG@K – rewards relevant results at higher ranks, supports graded relevance (highly vs partly relevant).

Example: top-5 results, relevant at positions 2 and 4, total relevant = 3 → Precision@5 = 2/5 = 0.4, Recall@5 = 2/3 = 0.67, reciprocal rank = 1/2 = 0.5. For LLM-based RAG evaluation without labels, use RAGAS context precision/recall.

What is prompt caching, and how is it different from semantic caching? How do you invalidate a cache?
Asked: Google, Wipro
  • Prompt caching (provider-side): the LLM provider caches the processed prefix of a prompt (long system prompt, tool definitions, large documents). 138
What is the difference between multithreading and multiprocessing in Python? What is the GIL, and how does it affect GenAI backends?
Asked: TCS, Infosys
  • GIL (Global Interpreter Lock): in CPython, only one thread executes Python bytecode at a time per process. It simplifies memory management but limits CPU parallelism with threads. (Python 3.13+ offers an experimental free-threaded build without the GIL.)
  • Multithreading: multiple threads in one process, shared memory. Because the GIL is released during I/O (network calls, file reads), threads work well for I/O-bound tasks – e.g. calling LLM APIs, vector DB, HTTP requests in parallel. Not useful for CPU-heavy Python code.
  • Multiprocessing: separate processes, each with its own interpreter and GIL → true parallelism on multiple cores for CPU-bound work – PDF parsing, OCR, chunking, local embedding computation, data processing. Costs: more memory, inter-process communication overhead.

GenAI backend practice: most work is I/O-bound (waiting for LLM/API responses) → use async (asyncio/FastAPI) or threads; push CPU-heavy ingestion to process pools or separate workers; heavy model inference runs in optimized C++/CUDA code (vLLM, PyTorch) which releases the GIL.

139
Concurrency vs parallelism: why use async programming in an AI application? If you must call three independent APIs, would you call them one by one?
Asked: Infosys
  • Concurrency – handling many tasks in overlapping time (switching while waiting); one core can do it. Parallelism – tasks literally running at the same time on multiple cores.
  • LLM apps spend most time waiting on network I/O. With async, while one request waits, the event loop serves others → much higher throughput with fewer resources; FastAPI is built on this.
  • Three independent API calls: don't call them sequentially (total = sum of latencies, e.g. 2 + 3 + 1 = 6 s). Run them concurrently (total ≈ slowest call, ~3 s):
import asyncio, httpx
async def fetch(client, url):
    r = await client.get(url, timeout=10)
    r.raise_for_status()
    return r.json()
async def main():
    async with httpx.AsyncClient() as client:
        weather, news, stock = await asyncio.gather(
            fetch(client, "https://api.example.com/weather"),
            fetch(client, "https://api.example.com/news"),
            fetch(client, "https://api.example.com/stock"),
            return_exceptions=True)          # one failure doesn't cancel the others
    return weather, news, stock
asyncio.run(main())

Only call sequentially when one call depends on another's result.

What are generators and decorators in Python? How do generators relate to streaming LLM responses?
Asked: TCS, Google
  • Generator: a function that uses `yield` to produce values one at a time, lazily, keeping its state between calls – memory-efficient for large/infinite data.
  • Decorator: a function that wraps another function to add behaviour (logging, timing, retries, caching, auth) without changing its code; applied with `@decorator`.
  • Streaming: LLM APIs return tokens as a stream; a generator yields each chunk as it arrives, and a web framework (FastAPI `StreamingResponse`, SSE) sends it to the user immediately → low perceived latency.
import time, functools
def timed(func):                       # decorator
    @functools.wraps(func)

        result = func(*args, **kwargs)
        print(f"{func.__name__} took {time.perf_counter() - start:.2f}s")
        return result
    return wrapper

142
Precision vs recall vs F1 score – when is recall more important than precision?
Asked: Google
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP) – of everything the model flagged, how much was correct. ("When I say fraud, am I right?")
  • Recall = TP / (TP + FN) – of all actual positives, how many did we catch. ("Did I catch all frauds?")
  • F1 = 2 × P × R / (P + R) – harmonic mean; useful for imbalanced data when both matter.

Recall matters more when missing a positive is costly: fraud detection, cancer screening, security threats, RAG retrieval (missing the relevant chunk means a wrong answer).

Precision matters more when false alarms are costly: spam filtering (losing real emails), blocking customer transactions, recommending content. Tune the decision threshold to trade them off; use PR-AUC for imbalanced problems; accuracy is misleading when 99% of transactions are legitimate.

What is overfitting, and what is data leakage? How do you prevent each?
Asked: Google
  • Overfitting: the model memorizes training data (including noise) – great training score, poor validation/test score. Prevent: more data, simpler model, regularization (L1/L2, dropout), early stopping, cross-validation, data augmentation, feature selection.
  • Data leakage: information that would not be available at prediction time leaks into training, giving unrealistically good validation results that collapse in production. Examples: fitting the scaler/TF-IDF on the full dataset before splitting; features computed from the future (e.g. "chargeback flag" when predicting fraud at transaction time); duplicates across train and test; random split on time-series data.

Prevent leakage: split first, then fit preprocessing on train only (sklearn Pipelines); time-based splits for temporal data; group-aware splits (same customer not in both); careful feature review.

  • Cross-validation (k-fold): train/evaluate k times on different splits for a more reliable estimate.

What is data drift vs model (concept) drift vs training–serving skew? Batch vs online inference?

  • Data drift: the input distribution changes over time (new product names, new customer segment, users start asking about a new policy) while the relationship may stay the same.
