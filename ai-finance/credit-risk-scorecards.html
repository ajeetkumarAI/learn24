Credit Risk Modelling & Scorecards

⏱ 10 min read✅ Check-yourself quiz🏦 Banking & fintech

Credit risk models estimate the chance a borrower won't repay. They decide who gets a loan, how much and at what price, so they are among the most regulated models in any bank. This lesson covers PD/LGD/EAD, scorecards with WoE and IV, and how ML fits in.

The building blocks

  • PD (probability of default): the chance of default (e.g. 90+ days past due) within a horizon, usually 12 months.
  • LGD (loss given default): the share of the exposure lost if default happens, after recoveries and collateral.
  • EAD (exposure at default): how much is owed at the time of default (credit cards: the current balance plus expected further drawdown).
  • Expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD. It drives pricing, provisioning (IFRS 9 / Ind AS 109 expected-credit-loss) and capital.

Classic scorecard workflow

  1. Define the target: "bad" = 90+ DPD within 12 months of the application; exclude indeterminate cases.
  2. Binning: split each variable into bins with monotonic bad rates (e.g. age, income, utilisation).
  3. Weight of Evidence: for each bin, WoE = ln(%good / %bad).
  4. Information Value: IV = Σ (%good − %bad) × WoE. A common rule of thumb is < 0.02 useless, 0.02–0.1 weak, 0.1–0.3 medium, > 0.3 strong (and check for leakage if suspiciously high).
  5. Logistic regression on the WoE-transformed variables.
  6. Scaling to points: score = offset + factor × ln(odds), often "20 points to double the odds" (PDO).
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
def woe_iv(df, col, target="bad", bins=5):
    d = df[[col, target]].copy(); d["bin"] = pd.qcut(d[col], bins, duplicates="drop")
    g = d.groupby("bin", observed=True)[target].agg(["sum", "count"]); g["good"] = g["count"] - g["sum"]
    g["pg"] = (g.good + .5) / g.good.sum(); g["pb"] = (g["sum"] + .5) / g["sum"].sum()
    g["woe"] = np.log(g.pg / g.pb); iv = ((g.pg - g.pb) * g.woe).sum()
    return g[["count", "sum", "woe"]], iv

Where gradient boosting fits

  • GBMs often beat scorecards on AUC/KS, especially with alternative data (transactions, bureau trends).
  • Requirements make them usable:
  • Monotonic constraints: e.g. higher utilisation must never lower risk.
  • SHAP-based reason codes: "declined because of high utilisation and recent missed payments".
  • Stability testing and documentation for validators.
  • Common hybrid: a GBM for ranking, plus a scorecard-style explanation layer, or a challenger model run alongside the champion.

Evaluation for credit models

  • Discrimination: AUC/Gini (Gini = 2·AUC − 1) and the KS statistic.
  • Calibration: predicted vs observed bad rate by score band.
  • Stability: PSI (population stability index) of the score and features over time; PSI > 0.25 is a common "investigate" threshold.
  • Vintage analysis: bad rates by origination month, to see how cohorts mature.
  • Reject inference: you only observe outcomes for approved applicants. Techniques such as parcelling or augmentation reduce that bias.

Fairness and regulation

  • Avoid protected attributes and proxies for them; test outcomes across groups.
  • Give adverse-action reasons to declined customers where regulation requires.
  • Follow the full governance cycle: documentation, independent validation, monitoring, periodic recalibration.

Check yourself

What is expected loss and how is it computed? EL = PD × LGD × EAD: the probability of default times the share lost if default happens times the exposure at default. It drives pricing, provisions and capital planning.
Why use monotonic constraints in a credit GBM? They enforce business logic (e.g. more missed payments can only increase risk). That makes the model stable, explainable and acceptable to validators and regulators.
What does PSI measure? How much a variable's distribution (or the score distribution) has shifted between a baseline period and a recent one. A large PSI signals population drift that may require recalibration.
