AI in Banking & Finance: The Big Picture

⏱ 10 min read✅ Check-yourself quiz🏦 Banking & fintech

Banks were among the first industries to use machine learning at scale. Credit scoring, fraud alerts and anti-money-laundering checks all run on models today. This lesson maps where AI is used in financial services, what makes the domain different, and which skills employers look for.

Where AI is used in a bank

Area Typical AI use Main model types
Payments & cards Real-time fraud scoring at authorisation Gradient boosting, graph features, rules
Lending Credit scoring, limit setting, collections Scorecards, logistic regression, GBMs
Compliance Anti-money laundering (AML), sanctions screening, KYC Rules + anomaly detection, entity resolution, NLP
Operations Document extraction, cheque and claims processing OCR, layout models, LLMs with structured output
Customer service Chatbots, call summarisation, complaint triage LLMs + RAG, intent classifiers
Markets Forecasting, risk models, research assistants Time series, NLP on news, LLM copilots
Marketing Next-best offer, churn, cross-sell Uplift models, recommenders

What makes finance different

  1. Regulation. Models that affect customers (credit, fraud blocks) must be explainable, validated and monitored. Regulators expect documentation, testing and governance. In the US this is guidance such as SR 11-7 on model risk management; in India the RBI expects similar discipline from regulated entities.
  2. Extreme class imbalance. Fraud can be 0.1% of transactions or less. Accuracy is useless as a metric, and precision/recall trade-offs map directly to money.
  3. Adversaries. Fraudsters adapt. When you block one pattern they move to another, so concept drift is constant.
  4. Delayed and noisy labels. A chargeback may arrive 30–90 days after the transaction, and not every fraud is ever reported.
  5. Cost asymmetry. A missed fraud loses money; a false decline annoys a genuine customer and may lose them for good.
  6. Privacy and data residency. Customer data is sensitive. Many GenAI uses need private deployments and strict access control.

The typical ML lifecycle in a bank

  1. Business case: define the decision, for example "block, step-up or approve a card transaction".
  2. Data: transactions, customer profile, device and channel data, bureau data, all with consent and lineage.
  3. Development: features, model, evaluation on out-of-time data.
  4. Independent validation: a separate team challenges assumptions, stability and fairness.
  5. Approval and deployment: a model inventory entry, monitoring plan and fallback rules.
  6. Monitoring: performance, drift (PSI), override rates, periodic revalidation.

Skills employers look for

  • Core ML: gradient boosting, logistic regression, imbalanced learning, calibration.
  • Feature engineering on transactions: velocity, aggregates, time windows.
  • Explainability: SHAP and reason codes.
  • Evaluation: cost-based thresholds, out-of-time validation.
  • Governance: documentation, monitoring, fairness.
  • GenAI with guardrails: RAG over policies, document extraction, human review.
  • Communication: explaining model behaviour to risk, compliance and business teams.

Check yourself

Why is accuracy a poor metric for fraud detection? With 0.1% fraud, a model that predicts "not fraud" for everything is 99.9% accurate and catches nothing. Use precision, recall, PR-AUC and the cost of each error instead.
Name two reasons fraud models drift faster than most models. Fraudsters adapt to whatever gets blocked (adversarial drift), and products, channels and customer behaviour change, for example new payment methods or festive-season spending.
What does independent model validation do? A team separate from the developers reviews the data, assumptions, performance, stability, fairness and documentation before the model is approved, and again at regular intervals.
